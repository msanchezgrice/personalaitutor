# Career Mapping: Turn Your Old Job Title into New Options

A job title can help someone recognize your background. It rarely describes everything you know how to do. When work changes, the useful question is which capabilities you can carry forward and what evidence you need for a new direction.

Career mapping connects past tasks to possible future work. Our career remapping approach adds a practical step: choose one gap, build an example, and use the result to revise your plan. Start with the map below, then adapt it to your own experience.

## 1. Map tasks before mapping titles

Write five statements beginning with “I did…” and use verbs that describe actual work. “I reproduced a difficult defect” is more useful than “I was in QA.” “I reconciled conflicting numbers before a report went out” reveals more than “I worked in analytics.”

For each statement, add the output, the judgment involved, and evidence you are allowed to share. If you lack an artifact, record the experience as self-reported. You can still investigate the connection without calling it independently verified.

Next, name the capability that may transfer. Reproduction work may involve test design. Reconciling a report may involve data validation. Explaining a release tradeoff may involve stakeholder communication. Keep the original task beside the proposed capability so the reasoning stays visible.

This gives you a skills map that you can correct. Remove a connection if it overstates your contribution. Add a missing task if the map overlooks important work. Keep uncertainty visible when the example is too vague to support a conclusion.

Those gaps are useful information. They help you choose between a nearby move and a larger retraining commitment.

## 3. Compare two directions using the same questions

Choose a primary direction and an alternative. Find current employer descriptions for both. Record the exact links and check dates, then compare responsibilities rather than relying on titles.

For each direction, ask:

- Which recurring tasks resemble work I have actually done?
- Which requirements have evidence, and which remain unassessed?
- What new domain or tooling would I need to learn?
- Does the work fit my interests and practical constraints?
- What small experiment would help me decide?

Avoid converting the answers into an unexplained match percentage. “Strong overlap in test design, scripting unassessed, location incompatible” is more useful than an apparently precise fit score.

You may find that neither direction fits. Keep that result. Revise the target or gather more information rather than forcing your history into the nearest available label.

## 4. Turn the largest useful gap into a project

Pick a gap that matters to the target work and can be investigated with a bounded task. The project should answer a question about your readiness or interest.

Consider this fictional example: Rin worked in games QA and reports writing reproduction steps and regression cases. Software QA is a plausible direction to explore. Rin wants to understand how those capabilities apply to checking AI-assisted outputs.

Rin builds a local checker using synthetic support replies. The project defines required fields, escalation rules, and examples that should be flagged. Development and evaluation cases remain separate. The result includes source files, run instructions, actual findings, and a limitation: checking saved replies does not measure the quality of a live model.

The completed project could support a narrow statement about building and explaining that checker. It would not establish that Rin is qualified for every evaluation role. Rin still needs to compare the work with the exact employer's requirements.

Use the same logic in other directions. A designer might test whether they enjoy workflow review. A marketer might learn an integration while preserving human approval. A product manager might discover that the difficult part is specifying recovery behavior, not generating the first interface.

## 5. Update your career story from the evidence

After the project, revisit the map. Which connection became stronger? Which gap remains? What did you discover about the work itself?

Keep three categories distinct:

- **Reported history:** work you say you performed.
- **Possible transfer:** a reasoned connection between that history and another task.
- **Demonstrated work:** a specific capability supported by an inspectable example under stated conditions.

Use the third category to support a project entry or interview example. Keep historical titles accurate and label independent work separately. If the project was self-reviewed, say so; if another person reviewed it, describe the scope of that review.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is career mapping?**

It is a way to connect experience, capabilities, possible roles, and development steps. Here, the map remains a hypothesis until you check it against real requirements and relevant work.

**What is the difference between skills mapping and career mapping?**

A skills map focuses on capabilities and evidence. A career map also considers possible work directions, interests, constraints, and the steps needed to investigate or pursue them.

**Can I map an old job title directly to a new AI title?**

Use titles to discover roles, then compare tasks and prerequisites. A title alone cannot establish a person's capability or qualification for a different job.

**What if I have no portfolio?**

Start with a task inventory. Create a small example using material you can share, document the result, and describe it accurately as independent work.

**Does a strong map mean I should apply?**

It can help identify a plausible role to assess. Check the current vacancy and your constraints, then make your own application decision. The map does not guarantee an interview or hiring outcome.
