# 12 Transferable Skills Examples You Can Back Up With Real Work

“Communication,” “problem solving,” and “leadership” can describe useful skills. On a resume, those words alone tell a hiring team very little. A stronger example explains what you did, the constraints you handled, and the output someone else could inspect.

Transferable skills are capabilities you can use across different jobs. [O*NET's transferable skills directory](https://www.onetonline.org/find/descriptor/browse/2.B/2.B.2) offers a broader reference for exploring them. Your next step is to connect a capability to your own tasks and the requirements of a particular role.

The examples below are fictional illustrations, not statements to copy as your employment history. Use the structure with work you actually performed. If you lack evidence for a claim, mark that gap and choose a way to investigate it.

## Twelve examples, from task to capability

| Past task | Possible transferable capability | Evidence you could describe |
|---|---|---|
| Reproduced a gameplay bug and wrote the exact steps | Systematic testing | A permitted bug report, or a fresh synthetic example |
| Compared a weekly dashboard with source records | Data quality checking | A reconciliation, assumptions, and corrected totals |
| Turned customer questions into a help article | Clear technical communication | The questions, article, and review changes |
| Chose which release issues to address first | Prioritization | Decision criteria and a tradeoff explanation |
| Revised a layout after usability feedback | Iterative design | Before and after screens with reasons for changes |
| Coordinated handoffs between two teams | Delivery coordination | A permitted checklist and ownership map |
| Explained an unusual campaign result | Analytical reasoning | The calculation and competing explanations |
| Interviewed users about a recurring problem | Discovery and listening | A consented, anonymized synthesis or synthetic practice set |
| Reconciled an inventory spreadsheet | Operational accuracy | Checks for duplicates, missing records, and totals |
| Wrote a brief another person could execute | Requirements communication | Acceptance criteria and a finished example |
| Trained a colleague on a repeatable process | Instruction and documentation | A training guide and the feedback used to revise it |
| Escalated a support question outside your authority | Judgment and boundary setting | An escalation rule and an explanation of why it applied |

None of these tasks automatically qualifies someone for a new title. A game tester may bring useful habits to software quality work while still needing to learn a different product, technical stack, or testing approach. The shared capability gives you a starting point for research and practice.

## Choose evidence before choosing impressive words

Start with five tasks from a typical week in your former role. Write each as a verb, an object, and a constraint. “Coordinated release handoffs across three teams under a fixed launch date” gives you more to work with than “strong team player.” Only use the team count if it is true.

For each task, record an output and a quality check. The output might be a report, layout, test case, schedule, or decision. The check explains how you knew it was usable: reconciling totals, reproducing a failure, reviewing accessibility, or comparing the result with written requirements.

Keep private employer material private. If you cannot share an artifact, describe the process at a permitted level or create a new independent example with synthetic inputs. A replacement example demonstrates your current work; it does not establish what happened inside a former employer.

## Compare capabilities with a target role

Take two current job descriptions from actual employers. Highlight the responsibilities rather than only the title. Then place each responsibility in one of three groups: work you have performed, work you have practiced independently, and work you have not yet demonstrated.

That distinction matters when AI enters the workflow. Writing good briefs may support learning an AI-assisted content process. It does not prove that you can check generated claims, manage permissions, or evaluate the final output. Those need their own examples.

Use the [career mapping guide](/learn/career-mapping-transferable-skills) to compare possible directions. A useful map shows both the connection and the missing evidence. It should become easier to revise as you learn more about the work.

## Turn one example into a resume statement

A useful structure is: **action, output, quality check, supported result**. You do not need a dramatic percentage. If a result was not measured, describe the output and its use accurately.

For a fictional completed independent project, that might be: “Built a report from synthetic support tickets, reconciled category totals against the source rows, and documented unresolved classification errors.” The claim describes observable work and admits a limitation.

For employment experience, preserve your actual title and dates. For new practice, use a separate Projects section. Never rewrite earlier work to imply that you used an AI tool before you actually did. The [resume guide](/learn/ai-skills-for-resume) explains how to keep those histories clear.

## Test one connection with a small project

Choose one important gap that is small enough to investigate this week. A former analyst could check an AI-drafted explanation against a synthetic spreadsheet. A designer could prototype a review screen and test its keyboard flow. A former games QA tester could build a small set of expected and unexpected replies and document which checks catch each error.

Keep the brief, inputs, steps, output, and review notes together. Write what the project demonstrates and what it does not. Self-review is useful practice, but label it as self-review. If someone else inspects the work, describe their actual feedback without turning it into a certification.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are good examples of transferable skills?**

Testing, analysis, clear writing, coordination, prioritization, design iteration, and sound escalation can transfer across contexts. Support each claim with a task, an output, and a quality check from your own experience.

**Can I list a skill if I do not have a public artifact?**

You can describe work you actually did without publishing confidential material. Explain the task accurately, respect permissions, and distinguish private employment experience from any new synthetic example you create.

**Does a transferable skill mean I qualify for a new career?**

No. It identifies a possible connection. Compare current role requirements, technical prerequisites, credentials where relevant, and your actual evidence before deciding what to pursue.
